## ARTICLE BODY (copy/paste to Substack)

I stood in socks in a Promontory living room last week staring at the Uintas through two stories of glass.

$8.7 million. 7,136 square feet. Dual masters. Chef kitchen flowing into a butler pantry. Aecum Development built it with Alexander Design. Across the street sits the Beach Club and the winter sledding hill.

You could hear people whisper about the steel beams and the white oak. I kept thinking about something else.

Who wired the money to build it?

That is the question nobody asks at the Showcase. After 16 years providing real estate services around Park City and Heber Valley, I can tell you it decides more than the tile does.

The 2026 Park City Showcase of Homes ran Aug 28 to 30 and Sept 4 to 7. The Park City Area Home Builders Association put 23 homes on ticket. Prices ran $2 million to $12 million. You saw Promontory, Tuhaye, SkyRidge Mountain Community at Mayflower Mountain, Marcella at Jordanelle Ridge, Jeremy Ranch, Silver Creek Village, The Aerie, Park City Heights, plus McKee Ranch, Saddle Creek, Winterton Farms and Charleston down in Wasatch County.

One Habitat for Humanity townhome sat in the mix. Everything else was mountain modern or alpine luxury. Ivory Homes had two entries. Aecum had two. Regal, Greenlaw, Mayflower Luxury Homes, Entrada, Jorgenson, Structura, Blue Ridge, Varuna, Timberidge, Menlove and PureHaven filled out the rest.

Walk five of those and a pattern shows up fast. Many were spec homes.

A builder found the lot. A money partner or three funded the build. The plan was simple. Finish in time for Showcase season, sell by ski season, split the upside.

On paper that is usually a joint venture or a small limited partnership. In practice I watch the same gap repeat.

The partners shook hands on economics. They never built the operation.

Then the finishes shine and the files stay dark.

### The pretty house problem

Spec work in Tuhaye or Promontory or SkyRidge costs real money before you sell. Lot at $800k to $1.5M. Vertical at $400 to $600 per foot for true luxury. Carry for 12 to 18 months. One change order on steel or glass can eat $90k.

So builders take on co-venturers. A surgeon in Salt Lake puts in $250k. A neighbor puts in $150k. The builder puts in sweat and a fee. Everyone expects a preferred return plus a split.

That structure looks friendly. Regulators see something colder.

If your money partners only write a check and wait for you to sell the house, they look passive under federal law. Passive means their interest looks like a security. The name on your LLC does not save you.

Securities attorney Tilden Moschetti puts it bluntly. The test is who does the work. If investors rely on your efforts for profit, you run a syndication even if your operating agreement says Joint Venture in big letters.

I have seen smart builders miss this completely.

They skip Form D. They skip accredited investor checks. They advertise the deal on Instagram under Rule 506(b) when that rule bans general solicitation. Or they use 506(c) and never verify anyone properly. They run without a private placement memo, without clear fee disclosure, without a debt cap, without a defined exit.

The SEC flagged exactly this in its 2026 exam priorities. Fee conflicts. Expense allocation. Valuation games on illiquid real estate. Marketing claims that drift from risk. Kelley Clarke Law summarized the 2025 to 2026 agenda the same way. Verification under 506(c) is getting fresh guidance, exemptions may get streamlined by April 2026, and exams are getting sharper, not softer.

You do not need a fraud case to get hurt here. You need one angry partner and one lawyer who knows rescission.

If you sold an unregistered security with no exemption, that investor can often demand money back plus interest, even if the house would have sold well. In Park City numbers, that can unwind a $3M spec in a week.

### The second risk lives in your bank feed

Say you got the exemption right. Most spec JVs still fail the basic transparency test.

I ask operators the same five questions.

Where is the partnership bank account? Show me the signed JV Terms. Show me who approved the loan draw. Show me the related party fee vote. Show me the audit log.

Silence. Then a Dropbox link with three PDFs named Final_FINAL_v2.

No connected bank account. No approval before debt or overruns. No log of who moved what and when. Customer deposits land in the builder operating account. The $100k acquisition fee never got disclosed. Distributions stop in month three while expenses keep running.

We lived a version of that in Florida. Five limited partners, $100k each into a beach hotel. $500k total. The manager took an undisclosed commission, broke the debt cap, went dark on statements, routed customer cash around the partnership account, and stacked tax allocations to himself. Pay stopped. Trust broke in 90 days.

Luxury spec has the same fault lines, only the kitchens cost more.

A Promontory build can carry 40 draws, 18 subs, two lenders, and three equity partners across two states. If approvals live in text threads and money lives in three accounts, you cannot prove what happened. Your CPA cannot close the books. Your investor cannot see risk. Your lawyer cannot defend you.

Substack figured this out for software. Real estate is next.

I have been reading the real estate SaaS writers here. The Real Estate Solutionist called 2026 the year AI moves from experiments to ROI in operations. Outcome Labs wrote in May that the software stack dies and the operating system wins. White Space Solutions mapped the 2026 investor stack piece by piece. The Startup Digest crowd calls it vertical AI eating SaaS.

They are all pointing at the same shift. Point tools collect data. Systems get work approved and logged.

Spec homes need that shift more than wholesalers do. One spec JV can hold $4M in risk with four people and zero staff. You cannot run that on memory.

### What active looks like on a spec

An active joint venture means every partner works and every dollar shows its receipt.

Real duties in writing. Real votes before related party pay. Debt, overruns, tax allocations, and distributions need approval before money moves. Customer funds route to the partnership account only. Every action lands in an audit log with date, amount, and receipt. Docs live versioned in one vault so dark books become impossible.

That is what we built the Joint Venture Operating System to do.

You create the company inside the system. You link the operating bank account and verify it. You generate JV Terms and a matching LLC operating agreement from one intake, then all partners sign. You log the first contribution with a receipt. Our live example logged $47,250 on March 14 for Blue Mesa JV LLC.

No passive seats. No vague splits. Flat fee stated upfront. You run your five checks before you fund the lot.

For a Park City spec, that flow is short on purpose.

Name, partners, roles, JV Terms draft. Bank linked. JV agreement signed. LLC agreement signed. First money in with a receipt.

Then the build can start. When a partner asks where things stand from Tuhaye or from Texas, you show one screen. Company, bank, docs, ledger. No thread hunt.

Pick one spec you loved. Ask the builder rep three questions. Is this home builder owned or client owned? How many capital partners funded it? Where do approvals and bank statements live?

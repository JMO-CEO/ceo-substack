## ARTICLE BODY (copy/paste to Substack)

I received a text message last Tuesday from a developer friend in Park City who just lost a capital partner over a $400,000 equity draw.

The text was short. We called it a joint venture in the operating agreement. Why is his lawyer demanding rescission?

Because the ink on the LLC agreement was dry, but the operation was dead silent.

After 16 years providing real estate services around Utah and watching hundreds of partnerships form and fracture, I can tell you that the legal title on your entity is worth zero when the SEC or an angry investor looks at the bank feed.

Sponsors across the country make the same mistake every day. They draft a document titled Joint Venture Operating Agreement, bring in three capital partners who write a check and wait for distributions, and assume the magic words protect them from securities regulation.

It does not work. 

Securities attorney Tilden Moschetti lays out the rule without flinching. The label on your document does not control the analysis. The economic reality does. If your investors write a check and rely entirely on your efforts to generate a profit, you are running an unregistered securities offering.

You are running a syndication. And syndications have rules that a simple DIY operating agreement ignores completely.

### The Passive Reliance Trap

The core test under federal law is deceptively simple. The Howey Test asks three questions. Is there an investment of money? Is that money pooled into a common enterprise? And do the investors expect to profit from the efforts of someone else?

In a true joint venture, every partner works. Every partner contributes operational effort, signs loan guaranties, makes binding decisions on renovation budgets, and actively manages the asset.

In a fake joint venture, the sponsor finds the deal, signs the loan, manages the construction, and deals with the tenants. The capital partners wire $250,000 each and wait for quarterly updates.

That waiting period is passive reliance. 

When your investors have no operational duties, no day-to-day control, and no practical ability to run the business, their interest is a security. It does not matter if you have known them for twenty years. It does not matter if there are only two of them. Small headcount and personal relationships do not exempt a passive deal from securities laws.

Sponsors pick the DIY label for one reason. They want to skip the Private Placement Memorandum, skip accredited investor verification under Rule 506, and save on legal fees.

That savings is an illusion. You save five thousand dollars on legal setup upfront and leave yourself exposed to full capital rescission on the back end.

### What Rescission Actually Cost Operators

When a partnership performs well, nobody checks the paperwork. Everyone cashes checks and talks about the next deal.

When a market softens, or a project hits cost overruns on steel and glass, the tone changes fast. A disgruntled investor who discovers the offering was unregistered can demand a full return of capital plus statutory interest.

Rescission acts as a put option for unhappy partners. They keep the upside if the project wins and hand you the downside if it fails. In luxury real estate numbers, that can unwind a multi-million dollar spec or multifamily build in a single week.

You do not need a fraud allegation to get hurt. You need one missing Form D filing, one unverified investor under Rule 506(c), and one lawyer who knows how to read partnership bank feeds.

### The Second Risk Lives in Your Bank Feed

Even when operators manage to structure exemptions correctly, most joint ventures fail the basic operational transparency test.

I ask operators the exact same five questions on our first call.

Where is the partnership bank account? Show me the signed JV Terms. Show me who approved the loan draw. Show me the related party fee vote. Show me the audit log.

Silence. Then a cloud link with three PDFs named Final_FINAL_v2.

No connected bank account. No formal approval before debt or overruns. No immutable log of who moved what and when. Customer deposits land in operating accounts. Acquisition fees never got disclosed in writing. Distributions stop in month three while expenses keep running unchecked.

We lived a version of that early in my career. Multiple partners, hundreds of thousands pooled into a single asset. The manager took undisclosed commissions, broke debt caps, and routed cash around partnership accounts. Trust broke in ninety days. Litigation followed.

Luxury development has the exact same fault lines. A project carries dozens of draws, multiple lenders, and equity partners across state lines. If approvals live in text message threads and money sits in mixed accounts, you cannot prove what happened. Your CPA cannot close the books. Your investor cannot see risk. Your lawyer cannot defend you.

Substack and modern software proved that transparency wins for publishing. Real estate operations are next.

Point tools collect data and leave it scattered across spreadsheets. Operating systems get work approved, logged, and timestamped before money moves.

### What Active Management Looks Like

An active joint venture means every partner holds real operational responsibility and every dollar shows its receipt.

Real duties in writing. Real votes before related party pay. Debt, overruns, tax allocations, and distributions need approval before money moves. Customer funds route to the partnership account only. Every action lands in an audit log with date, amount, and receipt. Documents live versioned in one vault so dark books become impossible.

## ARTICLE BODY (copy/paste to Substack)

I sat across from a developer last week who was trying to raise four million dollars for a spec build in Heber Valley.

He had gorgeous architectural renderings. He had a builder lined up with twenty years of framing experience. He had a signed three page term sheet scrawled on a coffee shop napkin.

I asked him one simple question.

Where is the partnership bank account?

He looked at me like I spoke French. He told me the money was going into his operating account so he could manage draws faster. He told me his investors trusted him. He told me they went to high school together.

Trust is not an audit log.

After 16 years providing real estate services and structuring developments across Utah, I can tell you that handshake capital is dead capital. When projects stall, trust vanishes in forty eight hours.

### The silent killer in real estate joint ventures

Most real estate joint ventures fail long before the first concrete truck arrives. They fail on structure.

A builder finds a great lot for one million dollars. Construction costs another two million. The builder has two hundred thousand dollars of cash and needs a capital partner to fund the rest.

They form an LLC. They split equity fifty fifty or seventy thirty. They shake hands and fund the project.

Then reality hits.

The first draw request comes in for ninety thousand dollars for steel beams. The capital partner wants to see invoices. The builder has receipts scattered across four text threads and a personal debit card statement. The capital partner asks why an equipment rental fee appeared without a vote. The builder says it was standard operating procedure.

Arguments start. Statements stop. Lawyers get involved.

The SEC and state regulators look at that exact setup and see an unregistered securities offering.

If your capital partner writes a check, stays home, and waits for you to sell the house so they can collect a return, they are passive under federal law. It does not matter what your operating agreement calls them. It does not matter if you call it a joint venture. If they rely on your managerial efforts for their profit, you are running a syndication.

If you ran that syndication without an exemption, without Form D filings, without accredited investor verification, and without a private placement memo, you are exposed.

I have seen smart developers lose everything over a rescission demand from a disgruntled partner. The house sold for a profit, but because the paperwork was flawed, the investor demanded their principal back plus statutory interest.

You do not need a market crash to get hurt. You need one partner who stopped trusting your text messages.

### The bank feed test

Even if your legal exemption is airtight, most spec joint ventures fail the transparency test.

I give operators five questions when they bring me a deal.

Show me the dedicated partnership bank account. Show me the signed joint venture terms. Show me who approved the last loan draw. Show me the related party fee vote. Show me the immutable audit log.

Four out of five times, I get silence.

Then I get a link to a shared folder with twenty PDFs named Final_Final_v2_revised.pdf.

No connected bank feed. No digital voting on overruns. No timestamped receipts. Customer deposits land in the builder personal account. Acquisition fees get paid without a recorded resolution. Distributions freeze in month four while overhead keeps billing.

We lived a version of that in commercial real estate years ago. Five investors put one hundred thousand dollars each into a retail strip. The managing partner took an undisclosed acquisition fee, mixed construction cash with personal funds, and stopped sending monthly statements. Trust broke in ninety days. Litigation lasted three years.

Luxury residential spec has the exact same fault lines, only the lumber packages cost more.

When you have forty draws, eighteen subcontractors, two lenders, and three equity partners across state lines, memory is not a management strategy. Spreadsheets are not systems.

Point tools collect data. Operating systems get work approved and logged.

Real duties in writing. Real votes before related party payments. Debt, overruns, tax allocations, and distributions require digital approval before funds move. Customer funds route directly to the partnership account. Every action lands in an audit log with a timestamp, user ID, and receipt.
